Getting Help for Gambling Problems

Understand the issue, review practical options and know what action can come next.

TALKPLANSUPPORT

Getting help does not have to begin with a perfect plan. It can start with one honest conversation, one practical barrier or one appointment that makes the next step easier.

1. Start with what is happening now

You do not need to diagnose yourself before asking for help. Begin with the facts you can observe: how often you gamble, how much money or time is involved, whether you are hiding it, whether you are chasing losses, and what consequences are appearing in daily life.

2. Choose a first point of contact

A trusted family member, friend, doctor, counsellor, specialist gambling service or support organization can all be reasonable starting points. The best first contact is often the one you are actually willing and able to use.

3. Prepare a few facts before the conversation

Write down recent deposits, losses, debts, time spent gambling, triggers and any limits you have tried. Having concrete information can make the conversation clearer and reduce the temptation to minimise what has been happening.

4. What support may involve

Support can include listening without judgement, screening or assessment, practical financial planning, counselling or therapy, peer support, self-exclusion, blocking tools, bank gambling blocks and referrals to other services when needed.

5. Add practical barriers while seeking help

Do not rely only on motivation. Stronger barriers can reduce access while you work on the underlying behaviour. Examples include self-exclusion, removing gambling apps, blocking gambling payments where available and asking a trusted person to help protect essential finances.

6. When the situation feels urgent

If gambling is creating immediate danger around money, housing, relationships or personal safety, prioritise immediate support rather than waiting for the situation to improve on its own. Use local emergency or crisis services when there is an immediate risk to safety.

7. Help can include the people around you

Gambling harm often affects partners, parents, children and friends too. Support services may also help family members understand boundaries, finances and how to support someone without taking responsibility for the gambling itself.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to stop gambling completely before asking for help?

No. You can seek support while you are still gambling. A service can help you decide what changes and safeguards are appropriate.

What if I am embarrassed to tell someone?

Embarrassment is common, but secrecy can make gambling harm harder to address. Starting with a confidential professional or support service can make the first conversation easier.

What should I take to a first support appointment?

If possible, bring a simple summary of gambling activity, debts or financial pressure, previous attempts to stop or limit gambling, and the main problems you want help with.

Can family members ask for help too?

Yes. Many gambling-support services provide information or support for people affected by someone else’s gambling.

Important: Responsible-gambling tools, treatment pathways and self-exclusion rules vary by operator and jurisdiction. Use official local services and current rules for your location.
