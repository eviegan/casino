House edge is the mathematical advantage built into a casino game over the long run. It is one of the most important concepts for understanding why casinos can operate profitably.

What is the house edge?

The house edge is the casino's theoretical long-term advantage, usually expressed as a percentage of eligible wagering.

If a game has a 2% house edge, it does not mean every player loses exactly 2% in a short session. It describes the long-term mathematical expectation across a large volume of play.

A simple house-edge example

Imagine $1,000,000 is wagered on a theoretical game with a 2% house edge. Over a sufficiently large statistical sample, the expected casino advantage would be approximately $20,000.

Individual players can still win or lose much more in the short term because of variance.

In a simplified model, RTP + house edge equals 100%.

Why house edge differs by game

Different games use different mathematics. Roulette depends on wheel layout and bet type. Blackjack house edge depends on rules and player decisions. Baccarat varies by wager type. Slots usually use a fixed mathematical configuration.

This means “casino game” is too broad a category to have one universal house edge.

When player decisions affect the edge

In some games, especially blackjack and certain poker-style games, player decisions can change expected value. Poor strategy can increase the effective house advantage.

In pure chance games such as standard roulette, betting systems do not remove the mathematical edge built into the wheel.

Same casino, very different house edges

The phrase “casino advantage” can sound like one fixed percentage, but it varies enormously by game and even by bet within the same game. For example, Wizard of Odds lists different house-edge figures for Banker, Player and Tie bets in baccarat; the Tie bet is mathematically very different from the main two bets.

Rules also matter. Blackjack payouts, deck count and dealer rules can change the mathematical edge. Roulette changes depending on wheel format and available rules.

Expected loss: translating a percentage into money

A useful educational concept is expected loss. If $1,000 is wagered through a game with a 2% theoretical house edge, the long-run mathematical expectation is $20. This does not predict an individual session; actual short-term results can be far above or below that figure.

The surprising part: Time and turnover matter. Even with the same $100 starting balance, repeatedly wagering it creates more total exposure to the house edge.

How to interpret house edge professionally

House edge is an expected-value measure, not a prediction of what one player will lose in a short session.

1

Check the exact bet and rules: different bets in the same game can have very different house edges.

2

Use expected loss carefully: house edge × total qualifying action gives a long-run expectation, not a guaranteed session result.

3

Separate house edge from volatility: two bets can have similar expected value but very different short-term swings.

4

Account for strategy where relevant: in decision-based games, the quoted edge can assume optimal or near-optimal play.

Frequently asked questions

Does a 5% house edge mean I lose 5% every session?

No. It is a long-term theoretical expectation, not a guarantee for one session.

Is house edge the opposite of RTP?

In a simplified model, yes. A 96% RTP corresponds to about a 4% house edge.

Can strategy reduce house edge?

In some decision-based games, correct strategy can reduce the disadvantage compared with poor play.

Do betting systems beat house edge?

No betting progression changes the underlying probabilities of an independent chance game.

Which games have the lowest house edge?

It depends on rules and strategy, so game-specific documentation should be checked.
